When the Road Floods, Nobody Can See the Current

Notes from a conversation with a PWD road overseer, Valapad section

Every monsoon, roads under the PWD's Valapad section turn into water channels. But floodwater on a road is only part of the danger. Sometimes the water is also live.

Where it floods

The overseer named the trouble spots without hesitation:

  • Thriprayar–Kanjani–Chavakkad road: a stretch near Anthikad that floods badly
  • Muchoor: both the curve and the straight road
  • Peringottukara: the mosque and post office roads
  • Kandassankadavu–Thriprayar road: including the route via the eastern nada
  • Beach roads: Valapad, Kothakulam and Palapetty, where sea surges push water onto the roadside even without rain

Many of these roads sit below the houses beside them. Ponds on both sides fill up and spill over, and traffic stops.

The water that doesn't leave

Some roads drain quickly, but the pond-side roads don't. Water stays from late May through August, and on some roads until September or October. Even when the surface looks dry, the ground isn't: when workers dig about 60 cm down for a pipe, groundwater seeps up. One pond on the Peringottukara mosque road has resisted side-protection work for years, because it doesn't dry out even by March. Its water level stays at road level.

The hazard you can't see

Fallen electric posts and snapped cables hidden under floodwater have caused many shock incidents, according to the overseer.

You can't tell by looking whether the water is live.

Today the process depends on people. Someone feels a shock or spots a broken cable and reports it. KSEB switches off the supply completely, and PWD helps secure the spot. Traffic resumes only after a check, and that check sometimes finds no shock at all, so the shutdown was precautionary. It works, but only after someone has already been exposed or scared.

Who is responsible?

Her answer was clear: KSEB. It should stay vigilant in every season and supervise all roads regularly, because a broken cable is invisible to a child walking through the water. As she put it, this is about the value of a life.

What she thought of our idea

We described a small device fixed at ground level on walls in flood-prone spots, placed roughly 50 metres apart. The devices detect electric leakage in the water, confirm it with each other, and send an alert message to people. She called it a genuinely useful idea, because today no one can see the danger at a glance and this would make it visible.

Takeaway

The problem is real, it recurs every year, and officials already deal with it reactively. The gap is early, automatic warning at known blackspots. That is what we're building toward.
