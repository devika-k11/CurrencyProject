When the Road Floods, Nobody Can See the Current

Field interview with a PWD road overseer, Valapad section, Thrissur

Sangeetha K S

Overseer, Public Works Department (Roads), Valapad Section. She supervises road maintenance and works, and is among the first officials to respond when roads under her section flood during the monsoon.

Introduction

Every monsoon, several roads under the Valapad section of the Public Works Department become water channels. Floodwater is only part of the danger. Sometimes the water is also electrically live. To understand how the problem is handled on the ground, our team interviewed the section's road overseer. This article records what she told us.

Where the flooding occurs

The overseer identified the following locations without hesitation:

  • Thriprayar–Kanjani–Chavakkad road: a stretch near Anthikad that floods badly.
  • Muchoor: both the curve and the straight road.
  • Peringottukara: the mosque road and the post office road.
  • Kandassankadavu–Thriprayar road: including the route via the eastern nada.
  • Beach roads: Valapad, Kothakulam and Palapetty, where sea surges push water onto the roadside even without rain.

Many of these roads sit lower than the houses beside them. Ponds on both sides fill up and spill onto the carriageway, and traffic stops.

Water that does not leave

Some roads drain quickly. The pond-side roads do not. Water typically stays from late May through August, and on some roads until September or October.

Even when the surface looks dry, the ground is not. When workers dig about 60 cm down to lay a pipe, groundwater seeps up. Side-protection work on ponds is best attempted between January and March, but one pond on the Peringottukara mosque road has resisted such work for years. It does not dry out even by March, and its water level stays at road level.

The hazard that cannot be seen

According to the overseer, fallen electric posts and snapped cables hidden under floodwater have caused many shock incidents in flooded areas. The central problem is that a person cannot tell by looking whether the water is live.

Today the response depends on people noticing. Someone feels a shock or sees a broken cable and reports it. KSEB then switches off the supply completely, and PWD helps secure the spot. Traffic resumes only after the area has been checked. Sometimes that check finds no shock at all, so the shutdown was a precaution. The process works, but only after someone has already been exposed or frightened.

Who is responsible?

Her answer was direct: KSEB. She said it must stay vigilant in every season, whether or not current is flowing, and should supervise all roads regularly, because a child walking through water cannot know that a cable has snapped.

For her, this is a matter of the value of a life.

Her response to our idea

We described our proposed solution, Jeeva Jalam: small devices fixed at ground level on walls in flood-prone spots, roughly 50 metres apart. The devices detect electric leakage in the water, verify it with neighbouring devices, and send an alert message to the people concerned.

She called it a genuinely useful idea. Because no one can see the danger at a glance today, an early warning that makes it visible would be a real help.

Conclusion

The problem is real, it recurs every year, and officials currently deal with it reactively. The gap is an early, automatic warning at known blackspots. That is the gap Jeeva Jalam is designed to close.
