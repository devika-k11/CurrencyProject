Electrified Floodwater on Roads: A Field Interview with the PWD Valapad Section,Kerala

Theme: Disaster Management · Project: Vidyut Raksha

Sangeetha K S

Overseer (Roads), Public Works Department, Valapad Section,Kerala

Ms. Sangeetha supervises the maintenance and execution of road works within the Valapad section,Kerala.

Introduction

Each monsoon, several roads within the Valapad section of the Public Works Department (PWD) become waterlogged. Flooding alone is a serious disruption, but it is not the only hazard. Where electric posts have fallen or cables have snapped, floodwater may also carry live current. To understand how this risk is managed at present, Team Cygnus interviewed the section's Road Overseer. This article documents her observations.

Flood-prone locations

The Overseer identified the following locations as recurrently affected:

  • Thriprayar–Kanjani–Chavakkad road: a stretch near Anthikad that experiences severe flooding.
  • Muttichoor: both the curved section and the straight road.
  • Peringottukara: the mosque post office road.
  • Kandassankadavu–Thriprayar road: including the route via the eastern nada.
  • Beach roads: Valapad, Kothakulam and Palapetty, where sea surges push water onto the roadside even in the absence of rain.

Many of these roads lie at a lower level than the adjoining houses. Ponds on either side of the road fill to capacity and overflow onto the carriageway, which halts traffic.

Persistence of standing water

Some roads drain within a short time. Roads adjoining ponds do not. On these roads, water generally remains from late May through August, and in certain cases until September or October.

The ground remains saturated even when the surface appears dry. During excavation for pipe laying, groundwater has been observed to seep in at a depth of approximately 60 cm. The period from January to March is the most suitable for constructing pond side-protection. However, one pond on the Peringottukara mosque road has not permitted such work for several years, as it does not dry out even by March and its water level remains at the level of the road.

The concealed electrical hazard

According to the Overseer, fallen electric posts and broken cables submerged in floodwater have led to a number of electric shock incidents in flooded areas. The central difficulty is that the presence of live current cannot be determined by visual inspection.

The existing response is reactive. A person experiences a shock or notices a broken cable and reports it. The Kerala State Electricity Board (KSEB) then disconnects the supply completely, and the PWD assists in securing the location. Traffic is restored only after the area has been inspected. In some instances the inspection finds no live current, which means the shutdown was precautionary. The procedure is effective, but it begins only after someone has been exposed to the risk.

Roles in ensuring electrical safety

Asked about electrical safety during floods, the Overseer explained that electrical installations fall within the domain of the Kerala State Electricity Board (KSEB), while the PWD supports the response by securing affected locations and coordinating with the Board. She emphasised that continuous vigilance is essential in every season, since a fallen or broken cable may not be noticeable to passers-by, particularly children.

She stressed that public safety is paramount, as the matter concerns human life.

Response to the proposed solution

The team presented Vidyut Raksha, a network of compact devices fixed at ground level on walls in flood-prone locations at intervals of approximately 50 metres. The devices detect electrical leakage in the water, verify the reading with neighbouring devices, and send an alert to the concerned persons.

The Overseer described the concept as highly useful. Since the danger cannot be identified at a glance today, an automatic warning that makes it visible would be a significant benefit to the public.

Conclusion

The interview confirmed that electrified floodwater is a genuine and recurring problem, and that authorities presently respond only after an incident or report. What is lacking is an early, automatic warning at known blackspots. Vidyut Raksha has been designed to address this gap.
